HTML Aside Element: What Content Belongs Inside?

This article provides an overview of the HTML <aside> element, detailing the specific types of tangential content it is designed to hold. It explores how developers can properly distinguish secondary, supporting information from the main flow of a webpage, outlines common real-world use cases, and clarifies the semantic rules regarding its placement within different document structures.

The HTML <aside> element represents a section of a document whose content is only tangentially related to the content around it. In semantic web design, "tangential" refers to supporting information that can be conceptually separated from the primary text without diminishing the reader's understanding of the main message. If the content were removed, the core document should still read as a complete, coherent piece.

Several specific types of content are appropriate for the <aside> element:

  • Sidebars: Document-level or section-level sidebars that house supplemental tools, author biographies, social sharing widgets, or lists of related articles.
  • Callout Boxes and Explanatory Notes: Informational boxes that provide background context, fun facts, or caveats about a topic mentioned in the main text.
  • Pull Quotes: Key phrases or quotes excerpted from the main text and enlarged visually to draw attention, provided they duplicate rather than introduce original narrative.
  • Glossaries and Definitions: Clarifications of industry-specific jargon, footnotes, or vocabulary lists that support the reader without interrupting the main prose.
  • Contextual Advertisements: Promotional materials or sponsor banners that are distinct from the primary article text.

The scope of an <aside> depends entirely on where it is nested within the document. When placed inside an <article> tag, its content must be strictly tangential to that specific article, such as a glossary for a single blog post. When placed outside an <article> and directly inside the <body> element, the <aside> applies globally to the entire website, acting as a sitewide sidebar, a collection of global links, or general advertising.

Content that is vital to understanding the page does not belong in an <aside>. Primary navigation, the main narrative of an article, copyright footers, and crucial media elements should instead use dedicated semantic elements like <nav>, <main>, <article>, <section>, or <footer>.
