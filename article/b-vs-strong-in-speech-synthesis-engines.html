vs in Speech Synthesis Engines

While the <b> and <strong> tags produce an identical visual result in standard web browsers—rendering enclosed text in a bold typeface—speech synthesis engines treat them fundamentally differently. This article explains the semantic distinction between these two elements, how Text-to-Speech (TTS) and screen reader engines interpret them during auditory rendering, and why choosing the correct tag is essential for accessible web design.

Visual Styling vs. Semantic Meaning

The difference between <b> and <strong> stems from the HTML5 specification:

  • <b> (Bring Attention To): Represents text stylistically offset from the normal prose without conveying any additional importance, urgency, or emphasis. Examples include product names in an article or keywords in a document review.
  • <strong> (Strong Importance): Represents text with strong importance, seriousness, or urgency. It is an explicitly semantic tag intended to alter the meaning of the sentence.

How Speech Synthesis Engines Interpret <b>

Speech synthesis systems, including screen readers such as NVDA, JAWS, and VoiceOver, prioritize semantic meaning over pure styling.

Because <b> is defined purely as a stylistic choice without semantic importance:

  • TTS engines typically ignore <b> entirely during standard speech output.
  • The text is read with standard pitch, cadence, volume, and rhythm (prosody).
  • No auditory cues or pauses are inserted, ensuring the flow of speech remains natural unless the user has explicitly configured their assistive device to announce text formatting changes.

How Speech Synthesis Engines Interpret <strong>

Because <strong> represents semantic importance, speech synthesis engines are designed to recognize it as a cue to modify how the words are spoken:

  • Prosodic Alteration: Advanced TTS engines can translate <strong> into altered prosody, usually characterized by an increase in pitch, slightly slower speaking rate, or higher volume to aurally simulate human emphasis.
  • Mapping to SSML: Modern speech platforms (such as Amazon Polly, Google Cloud TTS, or Web Speech API implementations) often map the <strong> element directly to the Speech Synthesis Markup Language (SSML) <emphasis level="strong"> tag.
  • Auditory Punctuation: Some screen reader configurations announce "strong" or insert subtle acoustic pauses before and after the text to alert users to critical instructions, warnings, or highlights.

Practical Implications for Web Accessibility

Using <b> when you intend to convey importance means non-sighted users and consumers of synthesized speech will miss the intended urgency or weight of the content. Conversely, using <strong> purely to make text bold can disrupt the listening experience with unintended, unnatural changes in voice modulation.

To ensure optimal behavior across both visual browsers and speech synthesis engines:

  • Use <strong> when the content requires genuine emphasis or denotes critical information (such as warnings or core instructions).
  • Use <b> for stylistic bolding where no change in auditory inflection is needed.
  • Use standard CSS (font-weight: bold;) when bold styling is purely aesthetic and unrelated to content structure.
